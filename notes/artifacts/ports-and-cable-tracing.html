01

The one idea to hold onto

Everything below follows from a single distinction: the logical layer versus the physical layer.

A switch knows what it talks to — not what it's plugged into.

It can name the devices exchanging traffic on each port. It cannot see the passive things a cable passes through on the way — those have no chip and say nothing.

Logical layer — we read this

Ports, link state, speed, VLANs, PoE, the MAC and LLDP identity of whatever is on the far end. Live from the switch.

Physical layer — invisible to the switch

Patch panels, wall outlets, the cable run itself, and the exact port a jumper lands on. Only a hand-kept cable record holds this.

How tracing works

A trace answers: “start at this port — where does the connection go, and what's on the far end?” We build it from two live sources the switch already gives us.

One MAC on a port means one device is sitting there. Many MACs on a port means it isn't a device at all — it's the uplink, and everything else is reachable through it. That single rule is what tells an ordinary drop apart from the backbone.

06

What a trace can & can't reach

The honest boundary — and why the dashed hop above stays a ghost.

Visible now

  • The switch port and its live state
  • The device name (LLDP) on the far end
  • The MAC + VLAN of a silent device
  • Every device reachable through the uplink

Invisible to any switch

  • Patch-panel ports the cable lands on
  • The wall outlet / floor box
  • The physical cable run and its length
  • Which faceplate jack maps to which port
07

To trace a cable completely

A full end-to-end trace — switch, through every device and panel in between, to the desk — needs the layers below joined together. RackTrack already owns the live ones; the passive middle has to be supplied.

Patch panel & jumpersA cable schedule (which port ↔ which panel jack)Recorded & labelled by hand / DCIMManual record
Wall outlet & deskStructured-cabling labels (TIA-606)Physical labels + the same recordManual record
  1. Enable LLDP everywhere — every switch, AP, and phone that supports it. More neighbours name themselves, fewer show up as bare MACs.
  2. Add every managed switch to RackTrack. With each one reachable, a trace walks hop-to-hop across them automatically — switch to switch to the final device.
  3. Label the passive plant and keep a cable schedule — one row per cable: SW:Gi1/0/1 ↔ PP-A:07 ↔ Desk-14. This is the only way the patch panels and wall jacks ever enter a trace.
  4. Feed that record in (spreadsheet or DCIM) so RackTrack can stitch the live logical ends to the passive middle — turning “they're connected” into a true door-to-desk path.
RackTrack — Ports & Cable Tracing. Every live value here is read from the switch over SSH and parsed; nothing is invented. The dashed hops mark the passive plant a switch can never see — the one part that still needs a human-kept record.
