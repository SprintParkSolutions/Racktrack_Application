▲ HIGHEST PRIORITY ○ NOT STARTED

Where RackTrack keeps its data — and why that's risky

In one line: everything a customer creates lives on a single computer, with no spare copy anywhere.

◉What's happening today

Right now, all of RackTrack's information sits in two places on one single computer. The account information — who the users are, which company they belong to, their settings — lives in a database file called SQLite, which is really just one file on the disk. The scan photos themselves live in ordinary folders on that same computer's hard drive. That's the whole setup: one machine, one copy of everything.

!Why that is a serious problem — two separate dangers

Danger one: there is no backup. A backup is simply a second copy of your data, kept somewhere else, so that if the first copy is lost you can restore it. RackTrack has none. If that one hard drive fails — and hard drives do fail, it's a matter of "when," not "if" — then every customer's scans and every account are gone permanently, with no way to get them back. That is the kind of event a business does not recover from.

Danger two: a scan can be half-saved. When RackTrack saves a new scan, it does it in two separate steps: first it writes the photos to a folder, and then, a moment later, it records who owns those photos in the database. If the computer crashes or restarts in the tiny gap between those two steps, you're left with photos that belong to nobody — an "orphan." This isn't hypothetical: the code already contains special repair scripts that were written specifically to clean up orphans after they happened.

✓What "good" looks like

A proper setup fixes both dangers at once, and it does three things:

1. It uses a real database. The industry-standard one is called PostgreSQL. Its key trick is that it can save the photos and the ownership record as a single all-or-nothing action — either both succeed together, or neither does. A crash can no longer leave a scan half-saved. (The technical word for this all-or-nothing guarantee is a transaction.)

2. It backs up automatically. Every night, a complete copy of everything is written to a separate location — a different building, really a different data centre — so a dead disk can never take the data with it.

3. Someone actually tests the restore. This is the step everyone skips: you take a backup and practise bringing it back to life, to prove it works. A backup nobody has ever restored is just a hope, not a safety net.

→How we actually get there

The good news is you don't build this from scratch. Both big cloud providers — Amazon AWS and Microsoft Azure — offer a "managed database" where the automatic backups are simply a setting you switch on, and they run and store the copies for you. So this fix arrives naturally as part of moving to the cloud.

Effort: roughly two to three weeks of engineering work — moving the data across, rewriting the save step to be all-or-nothing, turning on backups, and doing that one practice restore. It is the single highest-value piece of work on the entire roadmap.

★Why this one fix matters far beyond safety

This isn't only about not losing data. You cannot earn the ISO 27001 or SOC 2 certificates without it. Those audits specifically check that a company backs up its data and can prove it can restore it. So this single fix does triple duty: it removes the scariest risk, it's a headline item enterprise buyers ask about, and it unlocks the certifications later. That's why it sits at the very top.

In one sentence: today a single dead hard drive could erase the whole product, and fixing that is both the most urgent safety step and the foundation the certifications stand on.
