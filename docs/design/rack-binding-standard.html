Rack Binding Standard

RackTrack · Standard · Draft 2

Rack binding standard

The rules for deciding which device on the network is which box in a rack photograph, written for an estate of thousands rather than for the three switches on our own wall.

8 September 2026 Draft for review Supersedes draft 1

The physical layer and the logical layer are maintained by different people at different times, and neither is authoritative about the other. The binding between them is the thing nobody owns. This standard makes that binding a record in its own right — dated, sourced, rated for confidence, and allowed to be absent.

The problem

The binding between the two layers is the part nobody maintains

Whoever racks the hardware owns the physical layer. Whoever configures it owns the logical layer. Both records are kept honestly, and both drift, because no single person is accountable for the line between them.

A photograph is a fresh observation of one layer. A scan is a fresh observation of the other. The join is inferred at the moment we need it, from whatever the hardware happens to publish — and what it publishes varies enormously from one estate to the next.

  • 01
    We treat position as a property of a device, so a device belongs to whichever rack somebody typed it into. Moves, swaps and renumbering all silently invalidate it.
  • 02
    We infer the join from shape — port counts and sizes. In a room of identical top-of-rack switches, shape distinguishes nothing.
  • 03
    Every result is presented with the same certainty, so a customer cannot tell which rows of a report to act on and which to check.
  • 04
    The matcher considers the whole estate. At five hundred devices that is not a matching problem any more; it is a filtering problem we have not solved.

The solution

Make the binding a first-class record, and rate what it rests on

Identity comes from the hardware and never from configuration. Position becomes a separate observation with a source, a date and a confidence, which can be created, contradicted and retired without touching the device.

Then match what the estate can actually support. A well-run estate needs almost no inference; a poorly-run one gets a smaller, honest answer rather than a confident wrong one.

  • 01
    Identity is what the factory set — chassis address, or a serial from the hardware inventory. Never a name, never a management address.
  • 02
    Every binding records which of eight sources produced it, and carries one of four confidence levels.
  • 03
    Narrow before matching: the system of record, then memory, then the topology graph, then physical constraints. Never the full estate.
  • 04
    Grade the estate itself. Tell the customer what their instrumentation can support, and what it would take to do better.

Draft 1 of this standard was written from measurements of three low-cost switches in our own office, which publish almost nothing. That is the floor of the range, not the middle of it. Enterprise hardware publishes a full physical inventory, a real hostname and a complete neighbour table, and a standard designed only for the floor throws all of that away. This draft is graded instead.

1

Scope

This standard covers how RackTrack establishes the identity of a device, and how it binds that identity to a physical position in a rack. It applies to every scan, every report shown to a user, and every write into an external system of record.

It is written for estates from a single comms cupboard to a multi-site campus of thousands of devices, and for hardware ranging from unmanaged desk switches to modular chassis. Where behaviour depends on what the hardware publishes, section 3 governs.

MUST, MUST NOT and SHOULD carry their usual meaning in a specification.

2

Terms

Box
One physical object visible in a rack photograph, occupying one or more shelves.
Shelf
A numbered mounting position in a rack, counted from the bottom. Written U1, U2 and so on.
Device
One manageable network element, identified under section 4.
Chassis address
The hardware address a device publishes as its own identifier. Set at manufacture, not changed by configuration.
Site
The boundary inside which management addresses are unique. Usually one building.
System of record
The external database the customer treats as authoritative for what is racked where. Usually NetBox or another DCIM.
Binding
A dated, sourced statement that a device occupies a shelf range in a rack, carrying a confidence.
Physical inventory
The tree of components a device publishes about itself: chassis, modules, ports, and how they contain one another.
3

Grade the estate before matching anything

Everything this standard permits depends on what the hardware in front of us publishes. That varies by an order of magnitude between a campus of enterprise switches and a cupboard of desk switches, and the product must know which it is looking at before it claims anything.

Tier A · Modelled

The estate is in a system of record

A DCIM holds the rack, the shelf, the face and usually the serial for every device. Enterprise data centres and any customer with a change process are here.

The photograph is not used to guess. It is used to verify, and to report where reality and the record have parted company.

Matching is a check, not a search

Tier B · Instrumented

The hardware publishes a full inventory

Cisco, Juniper, Arista, HPE and similar publish a serial per component, a containment tree, a real hostname and a complete neighbour table.

Identity is exact without a system of record. Position comes from stack order, the neighbour graph and the photograph together.

Identity certain, position inferred well

Tier C · Bare

The hardware publishes almost nothing

No inventory, no serial, no usable name, no location. Our own office rack is here, and so is most small-business gear.

Identity still works, from the chassis address alone. Position must be learned once from a person and then remembered.

Learned once, never guessed

3.1

Every scan MUST determine the tier of each device before binding it, from what that device actually answered, and not from an assumption about the customer.

One rack routinely holds all three tiers: a chassis at the top, access switches in the middle, and something nobody remembers buying at the bottom.
3.2

Tier MUST be recorded per device, not per estate, and reported alongside the scan.

It is the single most useful thing we can tell a customer about their own instrumentation.
3.3

A device MUST NOT be denied a stronger source because other devices in the rack are at a weaker tier.

Grading down to the weakest device in the rack is the mistake draft 1 made across the whole product.
4

Identity

What makes a device that device and no other. The ladder below is ordered, and the first rung available is the one used.

4.1

A device's identity MUST be its site paired with the first of these that the hardware provides:

  1. the chassis serial number from its physical inventory;
  2. the serial published in the manufacturer's own private tree;
  3. the chassis address it publishes over LLDP;
  4. the bridge address it publishes as a switch;
  5. the hardware address of its management interface;
  6. its management address, marked provisional.
Rungs 1 and 2 are exact and printed on the box, so they can also be read from a photograph. Rungs 3 to 5 are exact but invisible. Rung 6 is not an identity and is marked as such.
4.2

An identity, once established, MUST be stable across a rename, a renumber, a configuration reset and a move between racks.

This is the test for whether something is an identity at all. Every rung above passes it; nothing in the configuration does.
4.3

A management address MUST NOT be treated as an identity, except provisionally under 4.1 rung 6, and a provisional identity MUST NOT be written into a system of record.

The same private address answers in every building of a campus. An address is a way to reach something, not a name for it.
4.4

A name the device publishes MUST NOT be used as an identity, at any tier, including where it looks like a good one.

A disciplined Tier A estate has excellent hostnames — and they are still configuration, still copied, and still changed without anyone telling us.
4.5

A published name MUST be treated as absent where it equals the device's model string, or where it occurs on more than one device within the site.

Both our TP-Links answer with their model number. In a large estate, copied configurations produce the same effect at scale.
4.6

Model MUST be taken from the device's published object identifier or its inventory, and MUST NOT be parsed out of its description string where either is available.

Description strings are free text and vary by firmware release. The object identifier is exact and stable.
5

What counts as one device

The two layers do not agree on what a device is. On instrumented hardware the containment tree settles it, and that tree also carries physical order — which is a genuine bridge between the layers, not an inference.

5.1

Where a device publishes a physical inventory, that inventory MUST decide what is a device, what is a component, and what contains what.

It replaces every heuristic we would otherwise write for stacks and chassis, and it is a standard, not a vendor extension.
5.2

A stack MUST be recorded as one device per member. Where the inventory gives each member's position within the stack, that order MUST be aligned to the vertical order of the corresponding boxes in the photograph.

This is the strongest bridge we have below a system of record: stack order is physical order, published by the hardware itself.
5.3

A modular chassis MUST be one device over its full shelf range, with cards as components. Card slot order SHOULD be aligned to the visible slot layout of the box.

A chassis with forty-eight-port cards otherwise reports hundreds of ports against a single shelf and defeats every shape-based check.
5.4

A device occupying more than one shelf MUST record its whole range and its lowest shelf. Shelves within an occupied range MUST NOT be reported as free.

Free-space reporting is one of the things a customer will actually act on, so getting it wrong has a cost beyond the scan.
5.5

Two devices the network presents as one logical unit MUST be recorded as two devices, each with its own identity and its own binding.

They are two things to service, two things to fail, and frequently two different racks.
5.6

The count of boxes and the count of devices are independent. Equal counts MUST NOT be treated as evidence of a one-to-one pairing, and port count MUST NOT be read from a box whose ports do not face the camera.

A rack of stacked access switches has far more boxes than devices. A rack of chassis has far fewer.
6

Evidence, ranked

Every binding rests on exactly one of these eight sources. There is no ninth, and a binding with no source is not a binding.

RankSourceWhere it comes fromTierShown as
1ConfirmedA person on site placed this device at this shelf.AnyA fact
2ReportedThe hardware states its own rack and shelf through a management interface.A, BA fact
3ModelledThe system of record says this device is in this rack, at this shelf.AA fact
4RememberedThis identity was bound here before by rank 1, 2 or 3, and nothing has contradicted it.AnyQualified
5ReadA serial or asset tag was read off the box in the photograph and matches an identity.A, BQualified
6OrderedStack or slot position from the physical inventory, aligned to the visual order of boxes.BQualified
7AdjacentA direct neighbour, or a stack peer, of a device already bound in this rack.A, BQualified
8InferredPort count, size or model agreement, and nothing else.AnyQualified
6.1

Every binding MUST record its source, the date it was observed, and the identity it binds.

Without a date, a binding cannot be aged out, and a stale one is indistinguishable from a fresh one.
6.2

A binding MUST NOT be made on rank 8 alone where more than one device fits the box.

Two identical switches one above the other are separated by nothing a camera can see, in any estate at any tier.
6.3

Where two sources disagree, the higher rank wins, the lower is retained, and the disagreement MUST be reported as a finding in its own right.

In a Tier A estate this is the most valuable output of the whole product: the list of places the system of record has drifted from the room.
6.4

Where the customer operates a system of record as authoritative, rank 3 SHOULD be promoted above rank 2 by configuration, and the promotion MUST be visible in the report.

Which of the two is trusted is the customer's governance decision, not ours. But they should be able to see which one they chose.
7

Confidence, and saying nothing

What we are entitled to claim, given what the evidence supports.

ConfidenceRequiresHow it is presentedMay be written back
ConfirmedRank 1, 2 or 3Stated plainly, with no qualifier.Yes
ProbableRank 4, 5, 6 or 7Stated with its reason attached, and easy to correct in one tap.Yes, marked
PossibleRank 8, exactly one candidatePut as a question the engineer answers, never as an answer.No
UnidentifiedEverything elseLeft blank, and listed as unidentified with the reason.No
7.1

Every binding MUST carry exactly one of those four levels, and the level MUST be visible wherever the binding is shown.

A report where everything looks equally certain is a report a customer cannot act on selectively, which means they act on none of it.
7.2

Only confirmed and probable bindings MUST be eligible to write into a system of record, and probable ones MUST be marked as such at the destination.

Writing a guess into the customer's DCIM converts our uncertainty into their fact, permanently and invisibly.
7.3

A box with no binding MUST appear as unidentified, and a device bound to no box in this rack MUST appear as not in this rack. Neither is a failure of the scan.

Most racks hold patch panels, power strips and unmanaged switches that will never answer anything at all.
8

Narrowing at estate scale

Twenty boxes against five hundred devices is not a matching problem. It is a filtering problem, and the filters below are ordered by how much they cut and how much they can be trusted.

8.1

The candidate set for a rack MUST be built in this order, stopping as soon as it is small enough to match:

  1. devices the system of record places in this rack;
  2. identities previously bound to this rack;
  3. stack peers of any device already bound here;
  4. direct neighbours of any device already bound here;
  5. devices whose uplinks terminate on a device already bound here;
  6. devices drawing power from a manageable strip in this rack.
Filters 3 to 6 come from the logical layer but describe physical constraints. A stack cable is under three metres, so stack peers are in the same rack or the one beside it.
8.2

The site's full device list MUST NOT be used as a candidate set, at any tier or any estate size.

It is what we do today, and it is why the match degrades as the customer grows.
8.3

The size of the candidate set MUST be recorded and reported. Where narrowing leaves a set too large to match honestly, the correct output is unidentified plus a statement of why.

Telling a customer their estate is not modelled well enough to place a rack is a real, actionable answer. Guessing is not.
8.4

Narrowing MUST begin from any device already bound at rank 1, 2 or 3, and one such device in a rack SHOULD be enough to resolve most of the rest through 8.1 filters 3 to 5.

Racks are cabled internally. One certain anchor turns the rest of the rack from a search into a short walk of the topology.
9

Labels and printed text

9.1

Text read from a photograph is evidence about a position, not about an identity, unless it contains a serial or an asset tag that matches one.

A rail label describes the slot; an asset tag describes the device. Only one of them travels with the hardware.
9.2

The shelf part of a label MUST have known confusable characters repaired before use, so that UIS reads as U15.

Built and tested, with the cases that must be left alone — USB, UPS — covered.
9.3

A label naming no known device MUST be kept verbatim and shown as unknown. It MUST NOT be matched to the nearest similar name.

Near-miss matching on names is how one device quietly becomes another in a customer's record.
9.4

Where a label disagrees with the identity from section 4, section 4 wins and the label MUST be reported as stale.

Hardware is replaced far more often than stickers are reprinted. The stale-label list is worth money to a customer on its own.
10

Change over time

A binding is an observation with a date, so the difference between two scans is defined rather than inferred.

10.1

Positions MUST be observed fresh in each scan. A position from an earlier scan MUST NOT be presented as current, though it MUST be retained as a rank 4 source.

One tidy-up shifts every device up a shelf, and the network shows no change whatsoever.
10.2

Same identity, new position: the device moved. Update the binding, retire the old one with its date, and create no second device.

Possible only because identity is hardware, not the rack it was first entered into.
10.3

New identity, same position and management address: the hardware was replaced, and this MUST be reported as a replacement rather than absorbed silently.

A name-based match gets this wrong without ever saying anything, in every estate at every tier.
10.4

Known identity, new management address: the address changed. Update it and keep the device, its bindings and its history.

A renumbering project should not lose a single device from the record, and today it would lose all of them.
11

Presence and reachability

11.1

Presence in a rack and reachability on the network MUST be recorded separately and never inferred from one another.

The one rule that keeps sections 6 and 7 honest.
11.2

A device MUST NOT be bound to a rack on the strength of answering. Answering proves it exists, not where it is.

A healthy switch in another building answers exactly as well as the one in front of you.
11.3

A box with no reachable device MUST be recorded as present and unreachable, with the reason distinguished where possible: no management configured, wrong credentials, blocked by policy, or no power.

Four different problems with four different owners. Reporting them as one missing device helps none of them.
11.4

Where the phone cannot reach a management network, the server SHOULD attempt it, and the box MUST be marked pending rather than absent until one of them has tried.

Management networks are routinely unreachable from the wireless network an engineer's phone is on. That is good practice, not a fault.
12

What a well-run estate provides

Four things a customer can do that move them up a tier and make almost all of the above unnecessary. RackTrack SHOULD measure each one and report where the estate stands, because that report is useful whether or not they ever act on it.

Model the racks in a system of record

Rack, shelf and face for every device. This alone moves an estate to Tier A and turns every scan from a search into a verification.

Enable neighbour discovery everywhere

It is the entire basis of section 8. Without it a rack is a set of unrelated devices; with it, one anchor resolves the rest.

Fill in the location and asset fields

They are writable and almost always empty. A device that carries its own rack and shelf is a rank 2 source and needs no inference at all.

Give every device a unique hostname

We will not use it as an identity, and we should not. But it makes every report readable by the people who have to act on it.

13

Conformance

A scan conforms when all six hold. Each is checkable and a test can assert every one.

13.1

Every binding carries a source from section 6, a confidence from section 7, an observation date and an identity from section 4.

13.2

No binding anywhere rests on a published name, and no stored identity is a management address that is not marked provisional.

13.3

No box is bound on rank 8 evidence where more than one device fitted.

13.4

Nothing below probable confidence was written into a system of record, and everything probable that was written is marked at the destination.

13.5

Every box without a binding appears as unidentified with a reason, and every device without a box appears as not in this rack.

13.6

The candidate set was narrowed under section 8, and its size is recorded in the scan.

14

What it takes to get there

Nothing above describes the product today. Three changes, in this order; the first is a precondition for both of the others.

Make identity a hardware fact

Today a switch belongs to the rack somebody typed it into. It becomes a device with an identity from section 4, which racks and bindings refer to.

Sections 4, 10 and most of 13 rest on this alone.

Sections 4, 10 · start here

Make the binding a record

Source, confidence, date, identity. The matcher already computes a score; it does not say what produced it, and the report shows every row alike.

This is what lets a Tier A customer see where their DCIM has drifted.

Sections 6, 7 · matcher and report

Narrow before matching

System of record, then memory, then the topology graph, then power. Report the candidate count and refuse to guess when it stays large.

Until this exists, nothing here survives an estate of five hundred devices.
