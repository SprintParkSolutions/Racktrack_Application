01

What was actually changed on your network

One change in this entire body of work touches the physical office network. It is worth stating without hedging, because it is the honest centre of the question.

On 16 July at 13:55 UTC, a network object named Cloud0-Mgmt was created in the EVE-NG lab test.unl as net9, with type: pnet0. In EVE-NG, pnet0 is the bridge to the host's physical NIC. Two minutes later, Ethernet0/3 on all three switch nodes was attached to it.

Before that change the lab was islanded. The API had shown the existing "Net" cloud as type: bridge — a private, isolated bridge — so nothing in the lab could reach 192.168.1.0/24 and nothing on the LAN could reach the lab. After it, three virtual switches sat on your office segment with static addresses.

That was the point. It was tracked on your own build list as "Lab switches put on the real network so the app can reach them." It was not incidental, and it was not hidden.

03

What a bridged lab can plausibly do — and what it can't

Taking the claim seriously means naming the mechanisms that are real rather than dismissing it. Four are worth your admin's time. They are ordered by how likely they are to produce a complaint.

Most likely

BPDUs from two foreign bridges

The two L2 nodes run spanning-tree mode pvst and send BPDUs out e0/3 onto your segment. If the office switch port feeding the EVE-NG host has BPDU guard enabled, that port err-disables the moment the lab boots.

symptom: EVE-NG host drops off the network, port shows err-disabled
Likely if STP is active

Topology-change churn on every node boot

IOL config is volatile and these nodes were restarted often. Each boot is a link-up event and an STP recalculation on a segment shared with your production switch, and TCNs flush MAC tables — brief unicast flooding each time.

symptom: intermittent slowness, "flapping", MAC table churn
Possible

Four static addresses, no reservation

.60–.63 were assigned by hand on 192.168.1.0/24. If your DHCP scope covers that range, an address conflict with a leased client is possible and would surface as a duplicate-IP complaint, not a switch fault.

symptom: duplicate IP warnings, one host intermittently unreachable
Possible

Unexpected MACs and CDP neighbours

IOL nodes use aabb.cc00.* MAC addresses. Three or four of them appear and vanish on one access port. On a switch with port security or a MAC limit, that is a violation; on a monitored network it is at minimum an alert.

symptom: port-security violation, unknown neighbours in discovery

The loop, specifically — because it is the first thing an admin will ask

The three lab switches are cross-connected internally, so a second path back to the shared segment would form a loop. It does not, and this is checkable in the committed script: server/scripts/configure-lab.js makes Ethernet0/2 — the internal uplink — an access port in VLAN 10 or VLAN 20, not a trunk. VLAN 99 is never carried between the switches. Their only VLAN 99 path is the office segment itself, which means one path, which means no loop. CoreSW is a Layer-3 image: its e0/3 is a routed interface, so it does not bridge or send BPDUs at all.

That leaves the two Layer-2 switches as the only devices in the lab that put spanning-tree traffic on your LAN.

04

What was checked at the time

The storm question was not raised for the first time by your admin. It was raised sixteen minutes after the wiring went in, by the person doing the wiring, and it was measured rather than assumed.

16 July, 14:13 UTC — measured, not asserted

EVE-NG API responded in 0.07 s. The gateway at 192.168.1.1 showed 0% packet loss over five pings. 192.168.1.33 returned its SSH banner normally.

A storming LAN does not produce those three numbers. Whatever else is true, the network was healthy immediately after the change.

Two caveats, so this is not read as more than it is. It is a single point-in-time check on 16 July, not continuous monitoring — it says nothing about the weeks that followed. And it was taken from a Mac reaching the LAN over VPN, which is a worse vantage point than the switch itself.

One detail that belongs in the record: .33 showed ICMP loss in that same check, and it was attributed at the time to rate-limiting from an earlier port sweep — a scan run from this side. Its SSH banner came back normally, so the switch was healthy, but the sweep did put load on it.

No network complaint was raised to me at any point between 16 July, when the lab went onto the LAN, and 13 August, when your admin's concern first came up. That is a weak signal rather than proof — a problem nobody mentions is still a problem — but a storm or a loop is not usually something a network goes four weeks without noticing.

05

How to settle it, from the switch side

Every check below is falsifiable and takes minutes. Run them on the office switch that feeds the EVE-NG host — the answers are on that box, not in the application.

  1. Look for the fingerprint in the switch log

    If the lab did this, it left a record. Search around 16 July 13:55 UTC (19:25 IST) — the wiring — and around every subsequent lab node boot.

    ! on the office switch
show logging | include SPANTREE|BPDU|err-disable|MACFLAP|PORTSEC
  2. Check who the root bridge is, and who is claiming it

    If an IOL node ever won a root election, the bridge ID gives it away — the lab nodes use aabb.cc00.* addresses.

    show spanning-tree
show spanning-tree detail | include ieee|occurred|from
  3. Look for the lab's MAC addresses on the port

    Three or four aabb.cc00.* entries on the EVE-NG host's port is the lab. None means nothing from the lab ever reached this switch.

    show mac address-table | include aabb.cc
show interfaces status err-disabled
  4. Rule the address range in or out

    Confirm whether 192.168.1.60–.63 fall inside the DHCP scope. If they do, a conflict is a real candidate and has nothing to do with spanning tree.

  5. The decisive test — remove the lab and watch

    Shut the EVE-NG host's access port, or stop the lab nodes, and leave it that way for a working day. If the symptom continues, the lab is not the cause and the investigation moves elsewhere. If it stops, you have your answer and the fix is section 6.

What is needed back from your admin

Nothing above can be aimed without one thing: what the symptom actually is, and when it happened. Ports flapping, a switch rebooting, slowness, a management session dropping, a monitoring alert — each points at a different mechanism, and several of them have nothing to do with this lab at all.

"There is an issue" cannot be tested. A symptom with a timestamp can be, in an afternoon.

06

What to do regardless of the outcome

Some of this is worth doing even if the lab is cleared, because the current arrangement relies on spanning tree behaving well between equipment that was never introduced to it.

  • Give the lab its own VLAN on the office switch. Put the EVE-NG host's port in a dedicated VLAN with the management addresses inside it. The lab keeps working, and its spanning tree stops being your production spanning tree.
  • Turn off spanning tree on the lab's management ports — no spanning-tree vlan 99 on the two L2 nodes — so they stop offering BPDUs to a network that did not ask for them.
  • Reserve 192.168.1.60–.63 in DHCP, or move the lab's management addresses out of the leased range entirely.
  • Let the admin set the boundary. They own the segment; the lab is a guest on it. A five-minute agreement on which port, which VLAN and which addresses removes this entire category of dispute for good.
07

The conclusion, in plain words

The application never touched your switch. That is settled by the server's own logs: 103 attempts, zero connections, ever — and nothing at all on 11 August.

The lab is a different question, and the honest answer is different too. On 16 July three virtual switches were deliberately bridged onto your office LAN, because that was the only way the product could read a real switch. Two of them speak spanning tree, and they have been speaking it to your network ever since. The risk was identified at the time, mitigated with a separate management VLAN, and measured — the network was clean sixteen minutes later and no one reported a problem for the next four weeks.

So: not proven, not impossible, and testable in an afternoon. Section 5 is how.

The part that is fair

Your admin is right that equipment appeared on their segment without their sign-off. That is worth conceding directly and fixing with section 6, whatever the logs eventually say about the symptom.

The part that is not

"Caused by the app" does not survive contact with the log database. Every claim in section 3's table is checkable on your own machine in one query.
