Traceroute — what it's for, in plain English

Why it exists, what it does, and what that 8.8.8.8 is — with a real trace from the switch.

Why it exists — the problem it solves

When the network is slow, or something won't load, you know something is wrong — but not where. Is it your own router? Your internet provider? Somewhere far away? Traceroute finds the spot. It follows your data step by step to a destination and shows exactly which step is slow, or where it stops.

What it actually does

Your data doesn't jump straight to where it's going. It's passed along a chain of routers, each handing it to the next — like a relay race. Traceroute lists every router in that chain, in order, and how long each took to answer. Each router is one hop. So a traceroute is just the numbered list of stops your data makes on its journey.

So what is 8.8.8.8?

8.8.8.8, plainly

It's Google's free public DNS server — one of the most reliable, always-answering addresses on the whole internet. Think of it as a phone number you know always picks up. We use it as a known-good, far-away test target. (Cloudflare's 1.1.1.1 works the same way.)

Why trace to it? A traceroute is only useful when the target is far. Something on your own network is just 1 hop away — nothing to see. 8.8.8.8 lives out on the internet, so tracing to it reveals the whole path: your gateway, your provider, and every hop beyond. That's why we use it to show what a real traceroute looks like.

A moment where it actually helps

Say people complain the internet is crawling. You run traceroute 8.8.8.8 from the switch and see:

  • Hops 1–2 — your gateway and your ISP — come back in 1 ms. Fine.
  • Hop 4 suddenly takes 300 ms.

Now you know the slowdown isn't inside your building — it starts at your provider. You've turned "it's slow" into "it's slow right here." That's the whole point: it tells you where, not just that.

Read it top to bottom — that's the exact route your traffic takes to reach Google, and each one was fast (~10 ms), so the path is healthy. If any hop were slow, that's where you'd look.

Ping vs traceroute: ping just asks the destination "are you there, how fast?" Traceroute asks that of every router on the way. Ping = is it up; traceroute = where is it slow or broken.

Does it show every device in between?

This is the part people get wrong. Traceroute shows only routers — the devices that pass traffic between networks. It does not show the rest of the gear a connection physically runs through:

Device on the pathShown in traceroute?
RoutersYes — these are the hops
SwitchesNo — Layer 2, invisible; they don't count as hops
Servers / PCs it passesNo — only the final destination shows, never pass-through devices
Patch panelsNo — passive, no chip, invisible to everything
The physical cableNo
So for the goal of "track one device to another, and everything in between": traceroute will not do that. It maps the router path across networks — nothing physical, no switches, no patch panels. To follow the real path device → switch → patch panel → switch → device, that's the cable trace: it reads LLDP (to find the switches), the MAC table (to find the end devices), and needs hand-entered patch-panel labels (panels are passive, so nothing can discover them automatically). Different tool, different job.
