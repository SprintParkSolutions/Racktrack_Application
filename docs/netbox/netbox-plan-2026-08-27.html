RackTrack → NetBox: the plan

Internal working document · 27 August 2026 · not for the customer

Telemetry knows what is connected. The camera knows where it is plugged in.
Neither is complete. Together they are.

01 Read this first

The cable data RackTrack produces today is not real

Every scan writes a file called topology.json full of cable connections. Next to it sits cmdb_synthesis.json, and that file says plainly that the data was generated: the model numbers, the serial numbers, the IP addresses, and all 83 cable connections were invented to make the demo look finished. They were never read off a rack.

Meanwhile, on a real photo, the part that reads text off equipment comes back with "ocr_failed" and no make, no model.

Why this matters more than anything else here. The one thing the Slovak customer says they care most about is using the camera plus the network to work out and verify which cable goes into which port. That is exactly the part that is currently made up.

If you show them topology.json, it will look perfect. Then they will test it on their own rack, the cables won't match, and you lose the deal and the reference. Universities talk to each other. Don't do it.

The good news, in the same breath

The other half is genuinely real and genuinely hard. RackTrack really does find racks in a photo, work out which shelf (U position) each box sits on, spot individual ports, tell which ports have cables in them, and identify cable colours. And it really does log into live switches and read their neighbour tables.

What's missing is the join between the two. That join is the product. That's what this plan builds.

02 What the customer actually asked for

Strip out the jargon and it's six questions.

1. Do you plug into NetBox already?

They already record their network in NetBox and don't want a second system to maintain. The real question is: does RackTrack feed NetBox, or compete with it?

Answer: it feeds it. Never position RackTrack as a NetBox replacement — you'd lose immediately.

2. How do I get data out?

They're checking they won't be locked in. API, JSON, CSV. Easy to answer well.

3. Can you produce these exact things?

Their list — sites, racks, devices, ports, cables — isn't random. Those are the actual table names inside NetBox. They've read the manual. This is a technical buyer; treat them as one.

4. Can a cable come out with both ends?

The sharpest question in the letter. A cable with one end isn't a cable, it's a note. They're checking whether you have real connection data or just an inventory list with photos.

5. What does it cost?

20–100 racks, several buildings. They're sizing a budget for a tender.

6. Will you scan one of our real racks?

This is the actual decision point. Everything else is preamble.

"The key capability we are particularly interested in is the combination of computer vision with LLDP/CDP/network telemetry to determine and verify physical cable-to-port relationships."

They've understood the value better than most prospects. They know a camera alone can't reliably follow a cable through a tangle at the back of a rack, and they know the network alone can't see patch panels or which shelf anything sits on. They want both, fused. That instinct is right, and it's exactly the right thing to build.

03 Your network admin is right

He said he can only give you three switches. He's correct, and it isn't him being awkward — it's physics.

A managed switch is a small computer

It has its own address, you can log into it, you can ask it questions, and it introduces itself to its neighbours.

An unmanaged switch is a piece of wire with sockets

No address, no login, no software. You can't ask it anything, because there's nothing in there to ask. It's a plastic box that copies electrical signals between ports.

Three managed. Exactly the three he offered. Asking him for the login to a DGS-1024C is like asking for the password to an extension cord. Tell him he was right — it'll do the relationship good.

Urgent: "2 D-Link and 1 uplink" needs clearing up today

"Uplink" isn't a type of switch. It's the port that carries traffic up to the next switch — port 24 on yours, the blue cable. Every switch has one.

So I can't tell which boxes are actually in your target rack. That matters enormously: if the two D-Links in that rack are the unmanaged ones, you have only one manageable switch there — and switches learn about each other by talking to a neighbour. One switch talking to nobody produces nothing to verify. The demo would be dead before it starts.

Go photograph the front of every box and confirm the model numbers. Quick test: no console socket, no management button, and it never shows up in the firewall's device list = it's unmanaged.

But unmanaged doesn't mean invisible

You can't log into a dumb switch, but you're not blind to what's behind it:

  1. The managed switch above it gives it away. Its uplink port will show many device addresses on one single port. A normal desk port shows one. Fifteen on one port means "there's a dumb switch down there with about fifteen things plugged into it."
  2. The firewall knows every device — name, address and hardware ID — including everything behind the dumb switches.
  3. So you know what exists. You just don't know which socket each thing is in.

And that — which physical socket, on a switch that can't be asked — is precisely what a camera sees and the network can't.

04 Should you replace the unmanaged switches?

Yes, eventually — but it doesn't unblock this project, and it quietly removes your cheapest demo.

What you'd gain

  • You'd know which socket every desk PC is in — about 38 devices you currently can't place
  • You could split the network into proper zones everywhere (right now anything behind a dumb switch is stuck in one zone)
  • All five switches would introduce themselves to each other, giving a complete verified map
  • Change detection would cover every port instead of about 60%

What you'd quietly lose

Those two dumb switches are your live proof that the network alone isn't enough. Make all five managed and a sceptic fairly asks:

"If every switch is managed, why do I need a camera? Free tools already tell me which device is on which port."

You need a better answer than the dumb switches — and luckily there is one.

The better answer, which was always stronger: patch panels

A patch panel is passive. No electronics, no brain, no address — not because it's cheap, but because there's nothing in it to power. You cannot make a patch panel manageable at any price.

Switches introduce themselves straight through it as if it weren't there. The switch sees whatever is at the far end; it has no idea a panel sits in the middle, or which panel socket the cable lands on. No network tool will ever see a patch panel. A camera sees it instantly.

And look at the customer's list — they mention patch panels twice, as separate requirements. Universities have thousands of them.

Dumb switches are a gap money closes. Patch panels are a gap that never closes. Build the story on patch panels and replacing the switches costs you nothing.

Other things only a camera will ever see

Which shelf, which rack, which room — no switch knows where it physically is

Asset stickers — only an eye or a camera reads a printed label

Kit that's switched off, broken, or racked but not yet plugged in

Blanking plates, cable managers, rails, plain power strips

Equipment that doesn't announce itself — most servers and printers

What's actually there vs what the config claims — spotting the difference needs both, by definition

If you do buy: practical notes

  • You don't need powered ports (PoE). Both dumb switches serve desks and laptops; the phones and access points are on the TP-Links. Non-powered managed switches are much cheaper — don't let anyone spec PoE by reflex.
  • 38 desks to cover — so a 24-port plus a 16- or 24-port.
  • Keep two boxes, not one big one. Cheaper to consolidate, but your own network document lists switch redundancy as its top improvement, and one box doubles the damage when it fails.
  • Timing is the real recommendation: don't wait for this. Three managed switches is enough to build and prove everything. Buying and installing would push the customer reply out by weeks, and that reply should go this week.

Turn the swap into your best demo

Scan the rack before the swap. Replace the switches. Scan again.

RackTrack already watches for exactly this kind of change, so swapping a switch produces a "something changed" record automatically, with no new code. You get a real before-and-after showing the product spotting a physical change on its own — which turns a boring purchase into the single best thing you can show a customer.

05 What NetBox is, and why we must not fight it

NetBox is a free, open database for recording a network: the buildings, the racks, the boxes in the racks, the ports on the boxes, and the cables between the ports. It's the industry default, and it's very widely used in universities — which is why this customer has one.

The key point

NetBox doesn't go and find anything. It's a filing cabinet. Somebody has to fill it in, and today that somebody is a person with a clipboard walking the rack.

That is the entire business case for RackTrack, in one sentence: NetBox is the filing cabinet; RackTrack is the thing that fills it in without the clipboard.

How a patch panel is recorded — they asked specifically

In NetBox a patch panel has a back side and a front side, and each front socket points at a back socket. Then you record two ordinary cables:

  • Cable 1: switch port 12 → panel front socket 7
  • Cable 2: panel back socket 7 → the wall socket at desk 402

NetBox then works out the whole journey itself and can tell you the switch port reaches desk 402 — even though no single cable says so. Showing that trace running through a panel is a strong demo moment: it proves you understood their system rather than dumping rows into it.

Being honest about confidence, inside their own tool

NetBox already lets a cable be marked either connected (it's really there) or planned (it's proposed). So:

How we knowRecorded as
Both switches confirm each otherconnected — proven
Only the camera saw itplanned — proposed, please review
Camera and network disagreenot exported — goes to a review list

Their engineers can then filter for "planned" and review exactly the ones we weren't sure about. This turns "our camera isn't perfect yet" from a weakness into a feature. Engineers trust tools that show their working. They don't trust tools that are always confident.

And that last row is the most valuable of the three. A disagreement usually means someone moved a cable and didn't update the paperwork — which is the exact problem they're buying this to solve.

06 The office rack: what to do

Your instinct was right — configure it properly first, then scan. A half-configured rack gives a messy scan, and you'll waste weeks unable to tell whether a mismatch is a software bug or a cabling mistake.

For the network admin

Do thisWhy
Fixed address on each of the 3 managed switchesSo RackTrack can always reach them
Name each switch to match its physical stickerThis is the key that links the photo to the live data. Sticker and name must agree
Read-only monitoring access (SNMP v3)The main way we ask a device about itself
Turn on neighbour announcements (LLDP) on every portThe single most important line in this table. Without it there are no verified cables and no demo
Turn it on for the phones tooYour 20 desk phones then announce themselves — 20 free verified connections
Same clock source everywhereSo "port 12 went down at 14:02" means the same thing on every device
Write a description on every patched portCosts an afternoon; every port then documents itself, and it double-checks what the camera reads
Save the configurationOtherwise it's all gone at the next power cut

For you, before any camera comes out

This is the difference between a scan that works and one that doesn't.

  • Label every box on the front — printed, same text as the switch's name
  • Label both ends of every cable
  • Label every patch panel socket clearly enough to photograph — that's the only way to identify sockets on a panel that can't be asked anything
  • Give everything an asset sticker (SP-0001, SP-0002…)
  • Tidy the cables. A neat back can be photographed; a bird's nest cannot, by any software, ever
  • Stick a position marker at a known shelf so the software has a fixed reference — a big accuracy win for a 20p sticker
  • Light it. Most text-reading failures are actually lighting failures

The most valuable hour in the whole project

Before the first scan, walk the rack and fill in a spreadsheet by hand: every shelf, box, model, serial, socket and cable.

This is your answer key. Without it you can't tell whether a scan is right — you're guessing. With it, every accuracy figure you quote the customer is defensible. Don't skip it because it's boring.

07 Demo 1: deliberately small, completely real

One rack. One setup. Real data only. Narrow and true beats broad and fabricated.

The rule: nothing invented

If a value has no source, the field stays empty. An empty field is a fact — "this patch panel has no serial number you can look up". A made-up one is a liability.

But typing something in by hand is not inventing it

Invented = software made up a plausible value nobody ever saw.
By hand = a person looked at the thing and typed what was there.

One is a lie; the other is data entry. Demo 1 needs some hand-typing, because a patch panel is passive and there's nothing to ask — and patch panels are the whole reason this beats a network-only tool.

The rule isn't "everything must be automatic". The rule is every value must have a named source. If you can say where it came from, it belongs.

In

Site · rack · manufacturer · model · the boxes and which shelf they're on · serial numbers for the 3 managed switches · their ports · patch panel sockets · cables with both ends.

Out (for now)

Asset stickers · power strips and UPS · network zones and IP addresses · cable lengths · anything involving a second rack.

Demo 1 produces two visibly different kinds of cable in NetBox: switch-to-switch marked connected (proven from both ends), and patch panel runs marked planned (asserted, not proven).

08 Building it separately, so nothing breaks

Decision: build this as a separate project. Don't touch the shipping app.

The current RackTrack has testers on it and runs the live demo site. A new data model, a new collector and a new exporter is exactly the kind of work that breaks things sideways. Keeping it out of the shipping code costs almost nothing and removes all that risk.

The principle

The new build reads the old one's results. It never changes it.

Don't rewrite the camera side — it took months, it works, and two copies means maintaining two copies. The new project calls it.

It also solves the made-up-data problem without any risky surgery: rather than ripping the invented cables out of the live app, the new build simply never reads them.

Two things to be careful about

1. Where the data lives. An EU university, infrastructure records, photographs of a data centre. They will ask where it's processed and stored. Have the answer ready, and have a "runs on your own servers" option ready. Improvising this on a call can kill the deal.

2. Don't demo the invented cables. If you need something visual for the first call, show the parts that are real — finding racks and shelves, spotting ports in use, and live data from your own switches. Those are genuinely impressive and nothing there blows up under scrutiny.

On price

Charge per rack, per year, in bands, with a named academic discount. Per rack is what they already count in, it scales with their value, and it's easy to defend in a tender. Their 20–100 racks sits in the top band, but keep the entry band affordable — universities almost always start with one building and expand after a good first year.

Charge separately for on-site setup, extra integrations, running it on their own servers (likely needed here), and training. For the trial: a fixed fee, credited in full against year one. Free trials get treated as free; a credited fee gets treated as a commitment by both sides.
