Knowing Which Device

Once we know which rack it is, the next question is which network device is which box in the photo, and whether it is the one the records expect.

15 September 2026from the Rack Binding Standard, draft 2almost none built

The problem, in one breath

The physical rack and the network records are kept by different people, and the line between them is the part nobody owns. Today a device belongs to whatever rack someone typed it into, we tell one box from another by its shape (how many ports it has), which decides nothing in a room of identical switches, and every guess is shown with the same certainty. The fix is to take a device's identity from the hardware itself, rate every match, and be willing to say "not sure."

How we decide which device is which box

1

Identity comes from the hardware, never a name

A device is what the factory set: the address or serial burned into the chassis, which you can also read off the printed label. That survives a rename, a new IP address, a config wipe, or a move to another rack. A name someone typed, even a tidy one, is never an identity, because it is configuration and it drifts.

2

Grade the gear first

ModelledThe records already hold every device's rack and shelf. Matching becomes a check: the photo confirms it and reports where it drifted.
InstrumentedThe hardware publishes its own serial, parts list, and neighbours. Identity is certain without any database, and position reads well from stack order and the photo.
BareAlmost nothing published, like our own office switches. Identity still works from the chassis address; position is learned once from a person, then remembered.

One rack routinely holds all three, so the grade is judged per device, never for the whole customer.

3

Rank the evidence, strongest first

  1. A person on site placed this device at this shelf.
  2. The device states its own rack and shelf.
  3. The records say so.
  4. It was bound here before and nothing has contradicted it.
  5. A serial or asset tag read off the box matches a known device.
  6. Stack order lines up with the order of boxes in the photo.
  7. It sits next to a device we have already placed.
  8. Only the shape agrees: port count, size, model. Weakest.

Higher rank wins a conflict, the loser is kept, and the disagreement is reported as a finding of its own. In a well-run estate, that finding is the most valuable thing we produce.

4

Four honest outcomes, and only the sure ones are written

ConfirmedA person, the device, or the records said so. Written plainly.
ProbableA clear winner on good evidence. Written, but marked as probable, and correctable in one tap.
PossibleOnly the shape fits, and exactly one candidate. Put to a person. Never written.
UnidentifiedNothing fits, or several fit equally. Shown with the reason. Never written.

Writing a guess into the customer's records turns our uncertainty into their fact, so a guess never gets written.

5

Narrow before matching, never the whole estate

In an estate of five hundred devices, comparing against all of them is a filtering problem, not a matching one. Start from the devices the records place in this rack, then ones bound here before, then their stack peers and neighbours, then what draws power in this rack. If the shortlist stays too large to be honest, the answer is "unidentified," with the reason, rather than a confident wrong pick.

What is built today

Almost none of this. Right now a device belongs to the rack someone typed it into, matching leans on shape, and every result looks equally sure. The one rule already in place is repairing a misread shelf label, so U1S becomes U15. Everything above is the "sort out the devices inside" piece of the build plan, and the largest of the four.
