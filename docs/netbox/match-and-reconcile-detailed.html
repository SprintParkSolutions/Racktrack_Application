RackTrack Match and Reconcile

RackTrack · how it works

Matching a real rack to the record

When a rack is built or a device is added, the team writes it into their database: DCIM, NetBox, CMDB, whatever they are comfortable with. RackTrack reads the rack as it actually is today, matches it back to that record, and shows what has changed in between. Sections 01 to 07 find the rack and its switches. Sections 08 to 14 do the same for every device inside it.

Rack level at a glance

Which rack am I standing in front of, which of the hundreds of live switches belong to it, and what has changed since somebody last wrote it down.

01

The three layers

What RackTrack reads

The physical scan of the rack, and the live switch data from the logical layer over SNMP.

What it compares against

The database the team already keeps, where every switch is recorded with its IP, MAC, serial number and the rest.

The hard part

Telling which physical switch is which logical switch, when the logical layer holds hundreds of them.

What comes out

A frozen list of what changed, one admin approving it item by item, and the record written back through the API.

RackTrack works with three sources of truth at once, and none of them agree by default. That disagreement is exactly what we are looking for.

The physical layer is the rack in front of you, captured by a scan. The logical layer is what the switches say about themselves when you ask them live, through SNMP. The database is what somebody wrote down when the rack was built or last touched.

The database is the bridge. It already holds the information for every switch: IP, MAC, serial number and everything else that was recorded. So the route is not physical to logical directly. It is physical to database, and then database to logical.

The database is the fastest path, not the only path. When the record is current it settles the question in one lookup. When it is not, the live network becomes the source of truth, and the record is what gets corrected.

02

Where it gets hard

Scanning one rack is fine. The trouble starts when you line those switches up against a logical layer that holds hundreds of them.

You have scanned a rack with three switches in it. You also have a live view of every switch on the network. Nothing in either view says which is which, and guessing across hundreds of candidates is not an option.

So we cut the problem down before we solve it. Find the rack first. Once we know which rack we are standing in front of, the database tells us which switches belong to it, and the logical layer drops from hundreds to a handful.

That leaves one question, and the next section answers it: how do we find the rack?

03

The six-step ladder

Six steps, tried in order. Each one is the fallback for the one above it. We start with the cheapest and most certain identifier, and only climb down when it is not available.

A  ·  When there is an identifier to go on
01

Read the labels on the switches

Use when the switches carry labels.

Get the labels off the switches and match them to the database. That gives us the corresponding data for those switches. From there, match the logical layer switches on what the database holds for them: MAC, serial number, chassis ID and so on.

A printed barcode or QR code is the strongest version of this. It is scanned rather than read, so it does not depend on OCR being right about a smudged or angled label. Where racks and switches carry codes, this is the fastest and most certain path in the whole ladder.

  • QR / barcode
  • Label text
  • MAC
  • Serial number
  • Chassis ID
02

Fall back to the rack ID

Use when there are no labels on the switches.

Get the rack ID and match it to the database. The database gives us the switch information for that rack. Then filter on the login devices to match the logical layer to the physical one.

  • Rack ID
  • Login devices
B  ·  When there is no identifier, narrow the candidates
03

Fall back to the rack location

Use when no rack ID is found.

Get the location of the rack and filter the database by that location. Once the candidates are narrowed down, run the steps above on them.

Where the facility still has its original design drawings, this gets much stronger. Every rack was given an X/Y coordinate on the floor plan when the site was built, mapped one to one with a rack ID. If the capture device can report where it is standing - row and column, a beacon, or a fixed camera mount - that resolves to exactly one rack instead of a shortlist, and the ladder can stop here.

  • Floor-plan coordinates
  • Room
  • Rack location
04

Use OCR on the individual rack

Use when the location turns up many racks.

Use OCR to read the information off the individual rack. Filter on what it says, find that rack in the database, and then run the steps above.

  • OCR from the rack
05

Use what is inside the rack

Use when 2 or 3 similar racks are still in play. This is the worst case for the automatic path.

Use the rack's own contents to tell it apart: the devices in it, the units, the empty spaces, the cable information. Use that to pin down the exact rack in the database, then follow the steps above.

  • Devices
  • Units
  • Empty spaces
  • Cable information
C  ·  When nothing settles it
06

Hand it to a person

Use when nothing above worked and more than one rack is still a candidate.

Take human intervention. The person selects the rack manually, and the flow carries on from there exactly as it would have if a label had identified it.

  • Manual selection
04

When the record is wrong, ask the neighbours

Everything above assumes the database is roughly right. Sometimes it is not. A switch gets swapped and nobody updates the record, so the MAC we were told to look for does not exist on the network any more - even though a switch is clearly sitting in that slot in the photo. A direct match will find nothing, and the correct device is standing right there.

The way out is to stop asking the device and ask the devices around it. LLDP and CDP are advertised continuously between directly connected equipment, with no database involved. A neighbour - a core switch, an aggregation switch, the top-of-rack switch next door - reports the live chassis ID and port of whatever is physically cabled into it at this moment. That is true whether or not anyone updated the record, and it is still true when the device itself has no credentials we hold or speaks a dialect we cannot parse yet.

So the slot gets marked outdated, the neighbours are asked what is on that link, and the answer identifies the switch. The correction is then prepared and passed on. It is not written automatically - see the note at the end of the next section.

05

Compare, decide, write

The physical and the logical layers are matched. You know which physical switch belongs to which logical switch.

But you still do not know how that switch's current state lines up with the state sitting in the database. That is where RackTrack comes in.

Three things happen from here. We compare, we work out what changed, and we hand it to one person who decides.

  1. 01

    Compare

    We pull the rack's record out of the database and lay it beside the scan. Then we freeze the result, so it stops moving while people are looking at it. Nothing is written and nothing is decided. This is only the two pictures, side by side.

    Check how old the record is first. The database stores when it was last synchronised. If that is older than the site's expected refresh interval, we carry a stale marker through the whole comparison, so the admin knows whether they are looking at something maintained last week or last year.

    Why freeze it. A live list changes every minute. If the admin approves something on Monday, we need to know exactly what they were looking at when they did.

  2. 02

    Identify

    We name each difference in plain words, and we say how confident we are about it. Three switches moved shelf and the hardware still matches, so we are sure. One has the same address but different hardware inside, so it was replaced. One matches nothing in the record, so it is new. One we cannot place, because two switches in that rack look identical and nothing separates them.

    Drift sticks to the slot. When a position in the rack does not match its record, that slot is marked outdated and stays marked until something verifies it. Drift becomes a standing state on the rack rather than a finding that appears and disappears with every scan.

    Nothing is dropped here. The one we cannot place still goes forward, marked uncertain. Deciding what to do about it is the admin's job, not ours to hide.

  3. 03

    Pass it to the admin

    The engineer who scanned the rack does not push. The frozen list goes to the admin for that site, and they work through it item by item. Nothing is all or nothing.

    Why one person. However many people end up involved, there is still a single name against every change that reaches the customer's record.

Approve

It is clear enough to act on. The admin says yes.

Goes into the push

Raise a ticket

Somebody needs to go and look at the rack first. The admin picks who, and assigns it.

Comes back here when done

Reject

It is wrong, or it is not ours to change. The reason is kept.

Never written

When a ticket comes back, it lands on the same admin, carrying whatever the person found. They then approve it or reject it, exactly as above. A finished ticket does not write anything by itself.

Just before writing, we compare against the database one last time. Approve at ten o'clock, somebody edits the record at five past, push at ten past, and without that check their work is simply gone and nobody ever finds out. If anything has moved since we froze the list, we stop and show the admin what changed.

And then it is written. Create what is missing, update what differs, delete nothing. Everything that happened is recorded: what changed, who approved it, who worked the ticket, and when it went in.

EngineerScans the rack, checks the mapping, runs the comparison, hands it over. Cannot push.
AdminSees everything. Approves, tickets or rejects each item, picks who works a ticket, and pushes, for their own site.
Whoever gets the ticketGoes and looks at the rack, sorts it out, sends the answer back to the admin. Cannot push.
NobodyDeletes anything from the database. There is no way to, on purpose.

Where automatic correction fits

When the neighbour lookup identifies a switch with high confidence, it is tempting to write the correction straight back and let the record heal itself without anyone touching it. That is a real gain and we want most of it, but not at the cost of the rule above.

So we do the work and not the writing. The correction is prepared, filled in, and marked high confidence, and it still lands on the admin's list. They click approve instead of investigating. The speed is kept, and every change that reaches the customer's record still has one person's name against it.

06

What reaches the ticket list

Not every difference deserves a ticket. These are the ones that do, roughly in the order they should be dealt with.

High

Something is wrong with the hardware itself, or with who knows about it.

  • A device is in the rack and nowhere in the database. Something is on the network that nobody wrote down.
  • A device is on the record, gone from the rack, and unreachable. Removed with no decommission behind it.
  • A device was replaced. Same slot and same address, different serial inside. Asset, warranty and support records are now pointing at hardware that is not there.
  • The vendor or model is not what is recorded.
  • A device turns up in a different rack from the one on its record.

Medium

The hardware is accounted for. The record describing it has drifted.

  • The device moved shelf inside the same rack. The RU position on the record is stale.
  • The management IP changed.
  • The hostname changed.
  • The serial on the device does not match the serial on the record. Usually the trace of a swap that was never written down.
  • The record says planned or staged, and the device is installed and live.
  • The record says active, and the device is unreachable.
  • A patch panel or PDU is present and not recorded.

Low, and not really drift

The database has a gap and we can fill it. This is us giving them data, not them being wrong, so it is worth keeping out of the ticket queue and offering as a bulk fill instead.

  • No serial on the record, and we read one.
  • No asset tag on the record, and we read one.
  • No RU position on the record, and we measured one.
  • Model or vendor blank on the record.

Cabling, where it is in scope

Only meaningful once the record actually holds cabling. Where it does, LLDP gives us a second opinion on every link.

  • A cable is in the rack and not recorded.
  • A recorded cable is not physically there.
  • A port is recorded as connected and is empty.
  • LLDP says the neighbour is one device, the record says another.

What does not become a ticket

Three things that look like findings and are not.

  • The physical and logical layers disagree with each other. That is our confidence problem, not the customer's. It gets resolved before it leaves us.
  • A device we could not poll. Until we can tell whether it is powered off, unreachable or gone, that is a "cannot determine", not a change.
  • Everything from the very first scan of a record that was never accurate. That is a baseline, not a drift report.

Device level: one box, one record, one switch

What we start with

A crop of each box in the rack photo with its shelf, the rack's own records, and the live switches already filtered down to this rack.

What is different

A handful of candidates instead of hundreds. Weak clues count now, and the physical and logical layers can be compared directly.

The hard case

Two identical switches, one shelf apart, no labels. Everything above this tab stops there.

What comes out

A binding per box: which device, which shelf, from what evidence, on what date, how sure. Then the same differences list as before.

The rack is found. Now every box in the photo has to be tied to one record and one live switch, and that is a different problem from the one the ladder solved.

At rack level the danger was scale: hundreds of switches and nothing to say which was which, so only strong identifiers were allowed to speak. At device level the candidate set is the rack's own contents, usually somewhere between two and twenty devices. That changes the arithmetic. A clue that could never pick one switch out of four hundred can easily pick one out of five. Port count, height, the colour of an uplink cable: each one weak on its own, together decisive.

09

The label: what a person wrote on it

Somebody stuck it there so the next person could find the record. That is exactly what it is good for, and all it is good for.

A label is a claim made by a person on a particular day. It is the fastest route into the database, because that is what it was put there to be. It is also the least trustworthy thing about the hardware, because the hardware can change and the sticker stays. So the label is used in one direction only: label to record, then record to wire. The record says what serial or address this name should have; the live switch either shows it or it does not.

How it is read

  1. 01

    Find the sticker as its own object

    A sticker is flat, high-contrast print, usually on the left of the bezel or on the blanking strip above. It is not the same problem as panel print, and it should not be read by the same pass that reads port numbers. Today it is: one text pass over the whole photo, and every fragment of text is assigned to whichever box its centre falls in.

  2. 02

    Codes first

    Any barcode or QR is decoded before OCR is attempted. It either scans or it does not, and a scan is not a guess.

  3. 03

    Then the text, and then its shape

    An address looks like an address. A Cisco serial is three letters, four digits, four characters. An asset tag follows the customer's own pattern, which we learn from the first few racks. Anything else is a name.

  4. 04

    Match inside the rack first

    Exact match against the rack's candidates, then the label as the tail of a longer record name (A01 inside lon-r12-a01), then the site. An unknown label stays unknown and is shown verbatim. No nearest-name guess, ever. The only repair allowed is a known confusable inside a unit segment (UIS becomes U15), and USB and UPS are exempt from it.

  5. 05

    Confirm on the wire

    The record names a serial or an address; the live switch must show it. If it does not, the label is stale: the wire wins, the label is reported, and a re-label ticket goes on the list at the low tier.

The print: what the maker put on the panel

The wordmark is big because it is branding. The model number is small because it is a specification. That one fact decides everything about reading it.

The limit is pixels, not software

Measured on our own racks: crop height is the one variable that predicts whether the model comes back. In a 1U slice of a full-rack photo the model string is six to ten pixels tall. Upscaling, sharpening and binarising were tried as three presets and gave identical recall, because they cannot recover detail the camera never captured.

Worse than a miss is a confident wrong answer. One crop returned a model at 0.74 confidence when that string appeared nowhere in the text that was read. The same photo gives two different models under two engines. Another pass returned a printed asset label as a device's model. A blank field prompts someone to go and look. A confident wrong model gets written down, syncs onward, and is trusted.

What we do about it

  1. 01

    Two passes

    The rack photo gives boxes and shelves. It does not have to give the print. Every box whose print came back as make-only or nothing is routed into a close-up: one photo per device, a ten-second task, where the model string is hundreds of pixels tall instead of eight.

  2. 02

    Read against the list, not into the void

    The text is matched by edit distance against two small sets: the models the record says are in this rack, and the vendor catalogue scoped by the wordmark that was read. "USW Ro Mas" is two substitutions from "USW Pro Max". Known confusables (0 and O, 1 and I and l, 5 and S, 8 and B, 2 and Z) cost less than other substitutions.

  3. 03

    Read is not inferred

    A model is read only if the string is in the OCR text. Otherwise it is inferred: kept in its own field with its own confidence, shown as "probably a DGS-1010MP", and never written to a record.

  4. 04

    The switch's own answer wins

    sysObjectID and the inventory tables beat the print every time. The print is asked when the logical side cannot be read at all (unmanaged, no credentials, no route), or to confirm that this box is that switch.

  5. 05

    Serial from the front only when the maker printed it there

    A pull-out tag is identity. Anything else is not, and a serial the camera cannot see is left empty and said to be empty.

In RackTrack today

  • ExistsA three-engine reader (PaddleOCR, then RapidOCR, then Tesseract) over a catalogue of 117 vendors and 6,182 model strings; brand fuzzy match; a stopword list for panel words; a digit is required before anything counts as a model; the close-up endpoint answers in 1.5 to 2.5 s and the rack-wide pass in about 9 s since the two bugs fixed on 8 Sep; make and model precedence is a person's override first, then the print.
  • PartialThe close-up path already returns the identifier's own confidence and its alternates. The rack-wide path throws them away and re-derives a proxy from the OCR confidence.
  • MissingRouting make-only and failed boxes to the close-up automatically; edit-distance match against the rack's own candidates; the read / inferred split (today an inferred model lands in the same field as a read one); any serial or address pattern in the photo path.

Cables and ports: the fingerprint

A 48-port switch with cables in 31 of its sockets is carrying a 48-digit number on its face. The live switch reports the same number as which ports are up. No label, no print, no serial needed.

This is the one device-level signal that goes from the physical layer to the logical layer directly. Cables are the behaviour of the box, and behaviour is what a switch reports about itself. Two identical switches, same model, same firmware, no labels, one shelf apart: their port patterns are almost never the same, and when they are, the uplinks usually differ.

Physical, per socket

Cable present or not. Colour. Copper, fibre or DAC. Link LED lit. A flag label at the connector. Which cage an SFP sits in.

The camera

Logical, per port

ifName, ifOperStatus, ifAdminStatus, ifHighSpeed, ifAlias. The LLDP neighbour's name, port and chassis. Addresses learned behind the port. ifPhysAddress.

The switch, over SNMP

Database, per interface

Name and type. The cable, its far end, its colour and label. Front and rear ports on a patch panel.

The record

How the comparison runs

  1. 01

    Sockets to port names

    The camera gives a grid: row and column, left to right. Every model numbers that grid its own way. Cisco puts odd numbers on the top row and even on the bottom; many others count straight across. A small per-model port template turns a grid cell into a port name (Gi1/0/17). Without a template, counting still works: how many sockets are cabled against how many ports are up.

  2. 02

    Occupancy against link, socket by socket

    Cable and up: agree. Empty and down: agree. Cable and down: unknown, not a mismatch. A dark spare, a dead far end and a port somebody shut down all look like this. Empty and up: a camera miss, or a port on the rear. It is reported, and it counts against the candidate, lightly.

  3. 03

    Score across the shortlist

    Each candidate switch gets an agreement count. The winner has to clear the runner-up by a margin. If two switches have the same pattern, the uplinks decide; if the uplinks match too, the next rung: the beacon in tab 12, or a person.

  4. 04

    The uplinks are the sharpest digits

    Four cages, each populated or not, each with a fibre of a known colour or a DAC, each with a neighbour that LLDP names. Two switches rarely share all four. The record's cable for that port should name the same far end LLDP does, and when it does not, that is a finding of its own.

Honesty rules carried over from the switch reader: virtual interfaces are dropped so a 28-port switch is 28 ports and not 29; LLDP's own port numbering is translated through lldpLocPortId before any socket is compared; a table the switch did not offer is filed as "returned nothing", never as empty ports.

The silhouette: a model without a single readable character

The measured problem with the print is that the model string is eight pixels tall. The port layout is the whole width of the box. Forty-eight copper sockets in two rows, four cages on the right, a management block on the left with two LEDs: that pattern is a Catalyst 9300-48, and it is not a Nexus 93180, whose whole face is cages. The public NetBox device-type library carries front elevation images for thousands of models. Matching the crop's structure (block count, block widths, cage count, block order) against those templates gives the family from the rack photo, at rack-photo resolution, exactly where the print fails. It is matched as structure, not as pixels, so lighting and angle do not break it. The result is inferred (rank 8 in the binding standard) until the print, the switch, or a person confirms it.

Ask the box to show itself

Every enterprise switch and server has a locator LED: beacon on Cisco, locator on Arista and Dell, chassis beacon on Juniper, the blue UID button on a server. Light it from the console and the phone camera sees which box is flashing. For the case nothing else settles, two identical switches with the same port pattern, it is the strongest proof there is: the logical layer points at the physical one.

RackTrack never sends the command. The rule that we do not write to switches stands. Instead, Review asks the admin to press beacon on the candidate from their own console, and the phone watches the rack for the one that lights. The switch that answered is the box that flashed, and the binding is recorded as Confirmed with the beacon as its source. If the customer's console does not offer it, the rung is skipped and a person picks from a shortlist of two.

How the evidence adds up

Every rung produces evidence with a rank, and the ranks are the binding standard's eight, unchanged: Confirmed, Reported, Modelled, Remembered, Read, Ordered, Adjacent, Inferred. Higher rank wins a conflict, the loser is kept, and the disagreement is reported as a finding. Rank alone is not enough when two candidates carry the same rank, so within a rank the evidence is scored, and the winner must beat the runner-up by a clear margin. A tie is not resolved by picking the first one.

OutcomeWhenWhat happens
ConfirmedThe switch itself, a code, a beacon or a person said so, and the wire agrees.Stated plainly. Written when the admin approves.
ProbableLabel, print or fingerprint picked a clear winner.Reason attached, one tap to correct. Written when approved, marked at the destination.
PossibleOnly the silhouette or the shelf fits, and exactly one candidate does.Put to a person as a question. Never written.
UnidentifiedNothing fits, or more than one fits equally.Listed with the reason and the shortlist size. Never written.

Then each bound box is compared with its record and its switch, exactly as section 05 describes. The differences are the same five kinds: New, Changed, Missing, Conflict, Unconfirmed. They go to the same admin and are written the same way. The device ladder changes how a box gets its identity. It changes nothing about how a change reaches the customer's record.

What to build, in order

  1. 01

    The evidence card

    One record per box with every claim verbatim, its source, its date and its confidence. This is the binding standard's "binding" made concrete, and it makes rack case 09 work at device level.

    Needs: nothing new from the camera. A schema and a store.

  2. 02

    Close-up routing

    Every box whose print came back make-only or failed is put in front of the technician for one close-up before the chain moves on from the Physical step.

    Needs: the close-up endpoint that already exists, and a prompt in the Physical step.

  3. 03

    Constrained matching

    Label and print text matched by edit distance against the rack's candidates and the catalogue, confusable-aware, with read and inferred kept apart.

    Needs: the catalogue that exists, a small matcher, one new field.

  4. 04

    Port templates and the fingerprint

    A grid-to-port-name template per device type, then cabled sockets against ifOperStatus, scored across the shortlist. This is the piece that solves two identical switches.

    Needs: templates for the models in the estate, the per-socket occupancy the detector already produces, the port table the phone already reads.

  5. 05

    The silhouette

    Structure match against elevation images for the models in the estate.

    Needs: the device-type library, and a structural descriptor per crop.

  6. 06

    A button that says "ask the admin to light this one", and a camera view that watches for it.

    Needs: nothing on the switch side. One screen.

  7. 07

Device findings and edge cases

Device level adds findings to the tiers in section 06, and a set of situations of its own. As before, none of them changes the shape of the flow.

High

The hardware is not what the record says.

  • Replaced. Same shelf, same address, different serial or model inside.
  • Vendor or model not as recorded, from the print, the silhouette or sysObjectID.
  • A box that matches nothing in this rack, and nothing in the site after the whole database is searched.

Medium

The hardware is accounted for. Something written about it has drifted.

  • Moved shelf.
  • Stale label. The sticker names a device the wire says is elsewhere. Re-label.
  • Ports on the rear, face recorded as front.
  • Stack member order differs from the record.

Low, and not really drift

The record has a gap and the box filled it. Offered as a bulk fill, not a ticket.

  • No serial on the record, and the pull-out gave one.
  • No model, and the print read one. Read, never inferred.
  • No position, and we measured one.
  • Cable colours and flag labels the record does not hold.

Cabling, per socket

Only where the record holds cabling. Every line has two witnesses: the socket and the port.

  • A cabled socket with no cable on the record.
  • A recorded cable with no cable in the socket.
  • A port recorded connected and the socket empty.
  • LLDP names a different far end than the record does.
  • Copper where the record says fibre, or 1G where it says 10G.

What does not become a finding

Five things that look like findings and are not.

  • A dark cable. Socket cabled, port down. Unknown, not wrong.
  • A port up with no visible cable. Hidden by a bundle, or on the rear. Noted.
  • An inferred model. Shown as "probably", never written.
  • A label that parses as nothing. Shown verbatim as unknown.
  • LEDs dark and no answer on the wire. Present, unreachable, no power. Not missing.
