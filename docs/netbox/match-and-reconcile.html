RackTrack · how it works

Matching a real rack to the record

Somebody wrote the rack into a database when it was built. RackTrack looks at the rack as it is today, finds what changed, and one person approves each change before it is written back.

01

Three things RackTrack looks at

None of the three agree by default. The differences between them are the whole point.

The photoThe switchesThe record
What it is
The rack as it physically is today, from the aisle.
What each switch says about itself when asked live over the network.
What somebody wrote down when the rack was built or last touched.
What it gives us
Which boxes, on which shelf, how tall. The maker's name and model on the front. Stickers and codes. Which sockets have a cable, and its colour.
Serial number, model, hostname, address, every port and whether it is up, and who is cabled to it (LLDP).
Every rack and device with its serial, MAC, address, position and cabling, and when it was last synchronised.
What it cannot say
Which live switch a box is. A serial or MAC on the back. Whether a cabled port is actually in use.
Where the switch physically is. Anything about patch panels, PDUs or unmanaged gear, which cannot be asked.
Whether it is still true. It stays as written until somebody changes it.
Role
Says which rack this is and what is in it.
The truth about the hardware. When the record is wrong, the wire wins.
The bridge. It says which few switches belong to this rack, so only those are compared.

02

The six steps

Every scan goes through the same six, in this order. Nothing is written until step six, and nothing is written without a person.

What happensWhat it usesWhat comes out
01
Photograph the rackOne photo from the aisle with the whole rack in frame. A close-up of a single box only when the print on it is too small to read.
The phone camera and the U mapping that turns pixels into shelves.
Every box with its shelf and height, the text and stickers on it, and each socket marked cabled, empty or not visible.
02
Find the rackTry the rack ladder (section 03), cheapest and surest clue first.
Codes and labels, the rack ID, where it stands, print on the rack, what is inside it, and last of all a person.
The rack's record, and the live switches filtered from hundreds down to the few that belong in this rack.
03
Name each boxTie every box in the photo to one line in the record and one live switch, using the box ladder (section 04).
Shelf, code, sticker, print, shape, the cable pattern against live ports, the beacon, and last of all a person.
A binding per box: which device, from what evidence, on what date, and how sure. Confirmed, Probable, Possible or Unidentified.
04
Compare and freezeLay the record beside the photo and the switches. Freeze the result so it stops moving while people look at it.
The record's last-sync date against the site's refresh interval, so a stale record is marked stale all the way through.
A frozen list of differences, each one New, Changed, Missing, Conflict or Unconfirmed, with a confidence. A slot that does not match stays marked until something verifies it.
05
One admin decidesThe admin for that site works the list item by item. The person who took the photo cannot write to the record.
Approve, raise a ticket for somebody to go and look, or reject with a reason. A finished ticket comes back to the same admin.
An approved set, open tickets with a named person on each, and rejections with their reasons kept.
06
Write it backCheck the record has not changed since the list was frozen. If it has, stop and show the admin what moved.
The customer's own API. Create what is missing, update what differs, delete nothing.
The record updated, and a log of what changed, who approved it, who worked the ticket, and when it went in.

03

Which rack: the six rungs

One rack out of every rack the customer has. Each rung is the fallback for the one above it, and whichever rung finds the rack, what happens next is identical.

ClueHow it is usedMove down when
01
A code or label on the switches
A QR or barcode is scanned, a label is read, and either is looked up in the record. That names the rack and its switches in one step.
There are no labels on the switches.
02
The rack's own ID
Matched to the record, which lists the switches that should be inside.
No rack ID is found.
03
Where it stands
The room, or its coordinates on the original floor plan. A capture device that knows where it is standing resolves to exactly one rack.
Many racks share that location.
04
Print on the rack itself
Words read off the frame or the door, matched against the remaining candidates.
Two or three similar racks are still in play.
05
What is inside it
The devices, the units, the empty shelves and the cables, compared with the records of the racks still in play.
More than one rack still fits.
06
A person picks it
From the short list the rungs above left, never from the whole site. The flow carries on exactly as if a label had found it.
This is the end of the ladder.
Before rung 1Ask for the site and room first, so the same rack ID in two buildings cannot collide.
Not in the record at allRung 6 has a second choice: this rack is new. It is created rather than forced onto a bad match, and a new rack with everything new gets one approval, not twenty.
On a repeat scanThe saved match is reused and the ladder is skipped, until the rack stops looking like itself.

04

Which box: the eight rungs

One box out of the few in this rack. With a handful of candidates, weak clues add up, and a rung that does not settle it still shortens the list.

ClueWhat it provesMove down when
01
Which shelf it sits onand what was on that shelf last time
Provisional only. The shelf is where the record says the device is, not what the hardware says it is.
More than one record fits the shelf.
02
A code on the boxQR, barcode, pull-out service tag
Scanned, not read. Asset tag or serial straight to the record, then confirmed on the wire. Where the estate is tagged, this is the whole ladder in one step.
There is no code.
03
A stickerhostname, asset tag, address
Finds the record. Proves nothing about the hardware, because the sticker stays when the device is swapped. The switch must agree before it is trusted.
There is no sticker, or the switch disagrees with it.
04
The maker's printwordmark and model on the front
The hardware type, when the model string is actually read. In a full-rack photo it is eight pixels tall, so a close-up is taken. A guessed model is shown as "probably" and never written.
The print is unreadable, or read but shared by more than one candidate.
05
Its shapeheight, port count, cage count, layout
Rules out every candidate of the wrong shape and gives the model family without reading a character.
More than one candidate has the same shape.
06
Its cablescabled sockets against ports that are up
A 48-port switch with 31 cables is carrying a 48-digit number on its face, and the live switch reports the same number. This ties the box to the switch directly, and it is what tells two identical unlabelled switches apart. Uplinks are the sharpest digits. The winner must clearly beat the runner-up.
Two candidates show the same pattern.
07
The beaconlocator LED
The admin lights the locator LED from their own console and the phone sees which box answers. The strongest proof there is. RackTrack never sends the command itself.
This gear has no beacon.
08
A person picks it
From a shortlist of two or three boxes. Recorded as Confirmed with the person as its source, and the next scan starts from it.
This is the end of the ladder.

How sure we are

OutcomeWhenWhat happens
Confirmed
The switch itself, a code, the beacon or a person said so, and the wire agrees.
Stated plainly. Written when the admin approves.
Probable
A sticker, the print or the cable pattern picked a clear winner.
Reason attached, one tap to correct. Written when approved, marked as probable at the destination.
Possible
Only the shelf or the shape fits, and exactly one candidate does.
Put to a person as a question. Never written.
Unidentified
Nothing fits, or more than one fits equally.
Listed with the reason and the size of the shortlist. Never written. A tie is never broken by picking the first one.

05

What a match is made of

No single field is enough. Several are scored together, and the best candidate must beat the second best by a clear margin or the question goes to a person.

StrengthFieldsWhy it sits here
Strongest
Chassis serial number. Base MAC of the chassis. Model and part number. Vendor. Asset tag where it is set. The serial of each member in a stack.
Burned in at the factory. Does not change when the device is renamed, readdressed or moved.
Strong
Management address. MAC of each interface. Hostname. Platform and OS string. Port count and port types. Uptime.
Right most of the time, and wrong exactly when someone has been working in the rack.
Position
What the neighbours report over LLDP and CDP: the chassis, port, name and address at the far end of each cable, and the cabling pattern itself.
Advertised live between cabled devices with no record involved. The way in when the record is stale or the device itself cannot be polled.
Photo
Codes. Label text. A serial, asset tag or MAC printed on the front. Model name and logo. Port layout. Shelf, height and face.
Every field above needs a counterpart on this side to be matched at all.
Tiebreaker
Rack and shelf. Site, room and tenant. Which sockets are cabled and the cable colours. Free units and rack layout.
Changes whenever somebody does routine work. Separates two identical candidates, never proves one on its own.
Normalise firstFDO-2117-A0X9, fdo 2117 a0x9 and FDO2117a0x9 are one serial from three sources. MACs with colons, dashes or dots are one address. Everything is put in one shape before anything is compared.
When the record is wrongIf the MAC the record expects is not live anywhere, the slot is marked outdated and the neighbours are asked what is cabled into that position right now. The answer identifies the switch from the wire, and a correction is prepared for the admin.
Prepared, not writtenEven a high-confidence correction lands on the admin's list. They click approve instead of investigating, and every change still carries one person's name.

06

What the admin sees

Every difference lands on the one admin for that site. Three ways out for each item, and the list is sorted so the serious things come first.

ChoiceWhenWhat happens next
Approve
It is clear enough to act on.
Goes into the write. The record is checked once more just before, and if it moved, the admin is shown what changed.
Raise a ticket
Somebody needs to go and look at the rack first.
The admin picks who. The single point of contact for the rack is emailed. The answer comes back to the same admin, who then approves or rejects. A finished ticket writes nothing by itself.
Reject
It is wrong, or it is not ours to change.
Nothing is written. The reason is kept.

What is on the list

TierMeaningExamplesHandled as
High
The hardware is not what the record says, or nobody knows about it.
  • A box in the rack and nowhere in the record
  • A device on the record, gone from the rack, and unreachable
  • Replaced: same shelf, same address, different serial inside
  • A different make or model from the one recorded
  • A device turning up in a different rack from its record
A ticket, dealt with first.
Medium
The hardware is accounted for. What was written about it has drifted.
  • Moved shelf inside the same rack
  • Address or hostname changed
  • Serial on the device differs from the serial on record
  • Record says planned, device is live. Record says active, device is unreachable
  • A stale sticker naming a device that left
  • A patch panel or PDU present and not recorded
A ticket.
Low
The record has a gap and we can fill it. Not really drift.
  • No serial, asset tag, model or position on record, and we read one
  • Cable colours and flag labels the record does not hold
One bulk fill, not a ticket each.
Cabling
Only where the record holds cabling. Every line has two witnesses: the socket and the port.
  • A cabled socket with no cable on record, or the reverse
  • A port recorded connected and the socket empty
  • LLDP names a different far end than the record does
  • Copper where the record says fibre
A ticket, per socket.
Not a ticket
Things that look like findings and are not.
  • The photo and the switch disagree with each other: ours to resolve, never the customer's
  • A cable in a socket whose port is down: unknown, not wrong
  • A device with its lights off and no answer: present and unreachable, not missing
  • A model that was guessed rather than read
  • The first scan of a record that was never accurate: a baseline, not drift
Held back, or noted.

07

Situations that need a rule

None of these changes the shape of the flow. Each one adds a small rule at one point in it.

SituationWhat we do
A switch moved to another rack
Search the whole record before calling a device new here and missing there. Confirmed on the wire before it is called a move.
A switch swapped for a different one
Same shelf and same address with different hardware inside is one line, "was X, now Y", not a removal plus an addition.
A switch removed
An empty shelf is not proof. Removed only when the photo, the network and the record all agree, and then marked decommissioned, never deleted.
A box that is in no record
Check in order: a move, then a planned device, then a decommissioned one. Only then create it.
Two identical switches, no stickers, one shelf apart
Shelf, print and shape all tie. The cable pattern decides, then the uplinks, then the beacon, then a person with a two-item shortlist. Never guessed.
One switch on the network, three boxes in the rack
A stack. One device per member, in the order they sit in the photo, with a serial per member where the switch offers it.
Patch panel, PDU, unmanaged switch
Nothing to ask, ever. Bound by shelf, sticker and record only. Never called unreachable, because there was nothing to reach.
Ports on the back, blank front
The face is recorded as rear. A rear photo is taken and the cable pattern runs on that.
Sockets hidden behind a cable bundle
Marked "not visible", never "empty". They drop out of the cable pattern instead of counting against a candidate.
The sticker says one thing, the switch says another
The wire wins. The sticker is reported as stale and a re-label ticket goes on the list at the low tier.
The photo is at an angle and the top shelves are small
A close-up is asked for on those shelves only, not the whole rack.
The customer has no database at all
RackTrack's own store is the baseline. Their database becomes an integration, not a requirement.

08

Six rules that never change

Whatever the ladder found and however sure it was, these hold at the point where anything reaches the customer's record.

1
Preview before write. The preview is exactly what happens.
2
Never delete. A missing device is marked offline only after a person ticks it.
3
Nothing invented. A value no source stated stays blank. A guessed model is shown as "probably" and never written.
4
Conflicts are never written. If the photo and the switch disagree, the item waits for a person.
5
One admin per site. One name against every change that reaches the record.
6
RackTrack never writes to a switch. Not even to blink a light.

The two ladders change how a rack and a box get their identity. They change nothing about how a change reaches the customer's record: the same list, the same admin, the same write.
